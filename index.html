How to spot a phishing email before it costs you

Phishing is a message that pretends to be someone you trust in order to steal passwords or money. Most phishing emails share the same few warning signs, and once you know them they are easy to notice.

What is phishing?

Phishing is a scam where an attacker sends an email, text or message that looks like it comes from a bank, employer or well-known service. The goal is to make you click a fake link, open an infected file or enter your login details on a fake page.

Six warning signs of a phishing email

No single sign proves a message is fake, but two or three together are a strong signal.

  • The sender address does not match the company name, or has a small spelling change.
  • The message creates urgency, such as "your account will be closed today".
  • It uses a generic greeting instead of your name.
  • Links show a different address when you hover over them.
  • It asks for passwords, one-time codes or card details.
  • There are unexpected attachments, especially ZIP or Office files.

How to check a suspicious message safely

Do not click anything in the message. Open the official website by typing its address yourself, or use the official app, and check for notices there. You can also call the company using the number printed on your card or on its real website.

What to do if you already clicked

Change the password for the affected account right away, and for any other account that uses the same password. Turn on two-factor authentication, check recent account activity, and tell your bank if you shared card details.

How a phishing attack unfolds

Knowing the sequence lets you break the chain at any step.

1. Research
Attackers collect names, job titles and company details from social media and old data leaks.
2. Bait
A message is written to look routine: an invoice, a parcel notice or a shared document.
3. Click
The target opens a link or file. This single action is where most damage starts.
4. Capture
A fake login page records the password, or malware installs quietly.
5. Takeover
The attacker logs in, reads email, sets forwarding rules and targets your contacts.

Business email compromise: the costly one

In this scam a criminal impersonates a manager or supplier and asks for an urgent payment or a change of bank details. It often has no link or attachment, so spam filters miss it. The defence is process, not software.

  • Confirm every bank-detail change by phone, using a number you already had.
  • Require two people to approve payments above a set amount.
  • Treat requests to keep a payment secret as a red flag.
  • Check the reply-to address, not only the display name.

Protecting family and older relatives

Scammers often target people who are less familiar with technology. A few shared rules protect the whole household.

  • Agree a family rule: nobody shares codes or sends money after an unexpected call or message.
  • Set up two-factor authentication on their email and banking together.
  • Teach the pause: real organisations will wait while you check.
  • Show them how to report scam messages in their email or phone app.

Frequently asked questions

Can phishing happen by text message or phone?

Yes. Text-message phishing is called smishing and phone-call phishing is called vishing. The same warning signs apply.

Is a padlock on a website proof that it is safe?

No. A padlock only shows the connection is encrypted. Scam sites can have it too, so check the address itself.
